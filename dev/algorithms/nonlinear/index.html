Nonlinear methods

A nonlinear method holds its configuration and nothing about the problem or the iterate. It enters the loop of solve!! through init_state, start!!, step!! and finish!!, so one method object serves each regime that its state supports, and the combinators can compose any of them.

Newton

Newton solves $J(x) d = -F(x)$ at each step and updates the iterate along d with its globalization. Its keywords are:

keyworddefaultmeaning
linsolveLUFactorization()the linear solver: a factorisation or GMRES
globalizationStatic()the globalization: a line search, DogLeg or LevenbergMarquardt
jacobiannothingthe Jacobian source: nothing (the problem's J, else AutoForwardDiff()), an ADTypes back end, a callable (J(x, p) on a value iterate, J!(J, x, p) on an array) except with GMRES, or a JVPFunction for GMRES
refinementNoRefinement()iterative refinement of the direction: Refinement or GMRESRefinement, see Mixed precision
regularization0the diagonal shift $\mu$ of $J + \mu I$
reusenothingthe factor-reuse period k
forcing, preconditionernothingthe forcing term and the right preconditioner of a GMRES
bestfalsereturn the iterate of the smallest $|F|_2$ of a solve that ends without SUCCESS
julia> using GeometricSolvers

julia> F!(y, x, p) = (y[1] = x[1]^2 + x[2]^2 - p; y[2] = x[1] - x[2]; y);

julia> prob = NonlinearProblem(F!);

julia> method = Newton(; linsolve = QRFactorization(), globalization = Backtracking());

julia> x, status = solve(prob, [1.0, 0.5], method, 2.0);

julia> x ≈ [1.0, 1.0]
true

Factor reuse

With reuse = k, a Newton step factorises the Jacobian and reuses the factor for up to k consecutive steps. It refactorises early after a STALLED step and after a failed refinement on a reused factor. QuasiNewton(k) is Newton(; reuse = k). A reused factor saves the evaluation and the factorisation of the Jacobian at the price of a linear rate, so it pays where those dominate the cost of a step.

julia> x, status = solve(prob, [1.0, 0.5], QuasiNewton(3), 2.0);

julia> status.code === SUCCESS && x ≈ [1.0, 1.0]
true

DogLeg and LevenbergMarquardt take no reuse, because their model needs the Jacobian at the current iterate.

Regularisation

regularization = μ factorises $J + \mu I$ instead of $J$. With an SVDFactorization a singular Jacobian gives the truncated minimum-norm step instead, and SolverStatus reports the numerical rank in rank.

The best iterate

A full Newton step can raise $\|F\|$, so a solve that ends without SUCCESS can end on an iterate worse than one it passed. With best = true, Newton keeps the iterate of the smallest $\|F\|_2$ among the start and every iterate a step returned, and returns it where the solve ends with a code other than SUCCESS and that norm is below the norm of the last iterate; status.fnorm then reports that norm, and every other field of the status is that of best = false. A solve that succeeds returns its last iterate. On $F(x) = x^2 + 1$, which has no real root, eight steps from 0.5 end on $\|F\| \approx 2.49$, and the sixth iterate has $\|F\| \approx 1.03$:

julia> noroot = NonlinearProblem((x, p) -> x^2 + 1);

julia> _, last_status = solve(noroot, 0.5, Newton(); max_iterations = 8);

julia> _, best_status = solve(noroot, 0.5, Newton(; best = true); max_iterations = 8);

julia> last_status.code === best_status.code === MAXITERS
true

julia> round(last_status.fnorm; digits = 2), round(best_status.fnorm; digits = 2)
(2.49, 1.03)

GaussNewton and QuasiNewton take the keyword too. A Deflated problem and the outer method of a BlockEliminated, a VariableProjection or a BatchEliminated problem refuse it, and so does LeftPreconditioned for a Newton in its outer method. The residual test can read the max norm instead of the 2-norm, with Options(T; norm = MaxNorm()) (Options); the ranking of best stays in the 2-norm.

GaussNewton

GaussNewton is a Newton with linsolve = QRFactorization() and Static(), for a LeastSquaresProblem. SVDFactorization serves a rank-deficient Jacobian. For a damped step, use Newton(; globalization = LevenbergMarquardt()), which takes any of the four factorisations. See Problem types for an example.

Picard

Picard is the damped fixed-point iteration $x_{k+1} = x_k - \omega F(x_k)$. It needs no Jacobian, and with $F(x) = x - G(x)$ it is the fixed-point iteration of G. The step converges where $I - \omega J$ is a contraction, and its rate is linear.

julia> fp = NonlinearProblem((x, p) -> x - cos(x));

julia> x, status = solve(fp, 1.0, Picard(; damping = 0.8));

julia> status.code === SUCCESS && isapprox(x, 0.7390851332; atol = 1e-8)
true

A line search (Backtracking, Bisection or StrongWolfe) along the Picard direction uses a measured slope. Anderson and Broyden accelerate a Picard iteration; see Composition and preconditioning.

Implicit differentiation

solve differentiates through a nonlinear solve. When x₀ or p carries a ForwardDiff.Dual, solve strips the duals, runs the primal solve to $x^*$, and on SUCCESS solves the tangent equation of the implicit function theorem,

\[J(x^*, p)\, \dot x = -\frac{\partial F}{\partial p}(x^*, p) \, \dot p,\]

from one factorisation of $J(x^*, p)$. The cost of the derivative is therefore one factorisation and one back substitution per partial, independent of the number of iterations of the primal solve.

julia> using ForwardDiff

julia> sq = NonlinearProblem((x, p) -> x^2 - p);

julia> dxdp = ForwardDiff.derivative(p -> solve(sq, 1.0, Newton(), p)[1], 2.0);

julia> dxdp ≈ 1 / (2 * sqrt(2.0))
true

A non-SUCCESS primal solve returns NaN partials. The accuracy of the tangent is that of the factor element type of linsolve. init and solve! refuse dual inputs. A StationarityProblem, a LeastSquaresProblem, a matrix-free GMRES solve, the combinators and the preconditioning methods have no implicit differentiation, and solve raises an ArgumentError for a dual input there. A Deflated problem differentiates the undeflated problem at the root it found.
