Composition and preconditioning

A combinator is a nonlinear method whose members are nonlinear methods. It enters the loop through the same init_state, start!!, step!! and finish!! as Newton and Picard, so a combinator runs in each regime that its members run in, and combinators nest. The composition follows Brune, Knepley, Smith and Tu (2015). The last two rows of the table are the preconditioning methods: RAS and LeftPreconditioned take a NonlinearProblem with a mutable vector iterate, so they run in R3 only.

combinatorone outer step
Additive(m₁, m₂, …)each member steps from the same iterate; the candidates are combined with weights
Multiplicative(m₁, m₂, …)the members step in sequence, each from the result of the last
Repeat(m, k)k steps of m
Switch(m₁, m₂; threshold)m₁ until $|F(x)| \le \text{threshold} \cdot |F(x_0)|$, then m₂ for the rest of the solve
Anderson(m, depth)one step of m, accelerated by Type-II Anderson mixing
Broyden(m, depth)one step of m, accelerated by Type-II Broyden multisecant updates
RAS(partition)restricted additive Schwarz: local Newton solves on overlapping blocks
LeftPreconditioned(outer, inner)outer on the residual $y - N(y)$ of one step N of inner

A combinator takes a NonlinearProblem or a StationarityProblem and refuses a LeastSquaresProblem. A combinator and a preconditioning method have no implicit differentiation. A combinator other than Repeat reports 0 in f_calls and jvp_calls, because its members count only their own steps.

The examples of this page use the fixed-point problem $x = \cos x$:

julia> using GeometricSolvers

julia> fp = NonlinearProblem((x, p) -> x - cos(x));

julia> root = 0.7390851332151607;

Additive

Additive runs one step of each member from the same iterate $x$ and forms $x_+ = x + \sum_i w_i (x_i - x)$. OptimalWeights chooses the weights that minimise the linearised residual $\|F(x) + \sum_i w_i (F(x_i) - F(x))\|_2$, with a safeguard that falls back to the best candidate; FixedWeights holds them constant. A member whose step fails hard is left out of the combination.

julia> x, status = solve(fp, 1.0, Additive(Newton(), Picard()));

julia> status.code === SUCCESS && isapprox(x, root; atol = 1e-8)
true

julia> x, status = solve(fp, 1.0, Additive(Newton(), Picard(); weights = FixedWeights(0.5, 0.5)));

julia> status.code === SUCCESS && isapprox(x, root; atol = 1e-6)
true

Multiplicative and Repeat

Multiplicative threads (x, F(x)) through its members in order within one outer step: Multiplicative(Picard(), Newton()) is a Picard step followed by a Newton step. Repeat(m, k) takes k steps of m per outer step. Between members, sync_merit passes the current merit to the line search of the next member.

julia> x, status = solve(fp, 1.0, Multiplicative(Picard(), Newton()));

julia> status.code === SUCCESS && isapprox(x, root; atol = 1e-8)
true

julia> x, status = solve(fp, 1.0, Repeat(Picard(), 3));

julia> status.code === SUCCESS && isapprox(x, root; atol = 1e-6)
true

Switch

Switch(first, second; threshold = 1e-2) runs first until the residual has dropped by the factor threshold, then latches to second. The common pattern is a robust method with a large convergence region first, and Newton for the fast local convergence after it:

julia> x, status = solve(fp, 1.0, Switch(Anderson(), Newton(); threshold = 1e-2));

julia> status.code === SUCCESS && isapprox(x, root; atol = 1e-10)
true

Anderson and Broyden

Anderson(member, m) accelerates the fixed-point map of member with Type-II Anderson mixing of depth m (Walker and Ni 2011). The history of differences is filtered by the length and angle filters of Pollock and Rebholz (2023) and the weight solve is regularised. The history restarts when the residual grows, and on a failed solve the iterate with the smallest residual is restored. Anderson(m) wraps Picard().

Broyden shares that history, its filters and its safeguards, and solves the lower-triangular Type-II Broyden multisecant system of Fang and Saad (2009) by forward substitution instead of the full normal equations. Broyden(m) wraps Picard(; damping = 0.5).

julia> x, status = solve(fp, 1.0, Anderson(Picard(), 2));

julia> status.code === SUCCESS && isapprox(x, root; atol = 1e-8)
true

julia> x, status = solve(fp, 1.0, Broyden(2));

julia> status.code === SUCCESS && isapprox(x, root; atol = 1e-8)
true

In R1 the depth is 1 ≤ m ≤ 4 and the state is isbits; in R3 any depth applies, with preallocated $n \times m$ difference matrices.

Restricted additive Schwarz: RAS

RAS(partition) is the one-level restricted additive Schwarz map of nonlinear preconditioning. A BlockPartition splits 1:n into overlapping subdomains, each with an owner set; the owner sets are disjoint and cover 1:n. One step restricts the iterate to each subdomain, takes up to max_iterations local Newton steps with that block's own factor, and writes back the owned indices only. The problem supplies one local residual F_i!(f_i, x_i, x, p) per block through the block_residuals keyword of NonlinearProblem, which reads the couplings outside the block from the frozen full iterate x. A block without its own Jacobian is differentiated with AutoForwardDiff().

julia> n = 8;

julia> cell(u, j) = u[j] * (1 + (j == 1 ? 0.0 : u[j - 1])^2 + (j == n ? 0.0 : u[j + 1])^2) - 1;

julia> F!(r, x, p) = (for j in 1:n; r[j] = cell(x, j); end; r);

julia> part = BlockPartition(n, 2; overlap = 1);

julia> blocks = ntuple(2) do i
           S = part.subdomains[i]
           function (f, x_loc, x, p)
               u = Vector{promote_type(eltype(x_loc), eltype(x))}(x)
               u[S] .= x_loc
               for (k, j) in enumerate(S)
                   f[k] = cell(u, j)
               end
               return f
           end
       end;

julia> prob = NonlinearProblem(F!; block_residuals = blocks);

julia> x_ras, status = solve(prob, zeros(n), RAS(part; max_iterations = 20, rtol = 1e-12));

julia> status.code === SUCCESS
true

With rtol or atol positive, a local solve that does not converge ends the solve with MAXITERS, and ras_counts names the block.

Nonlinear preconditioning: LeftPreconditioned

LeftPreconditioned(outer, inner) runs outer on the left-preconditioned residual $r^\ell(y) = y - N(y)$, where $N$ is one step of inner. The zeros of $r^\ell$ are the fixed points of $N$. Every trial point of the globalization of outer costs one step of inner.

  • LeftPreconditioned(Newton(), RAS(partition)) is RASPEN (Dolean, Gander, Kheriji, Kwok and Masson 2016). Its Jacobian is the exact form from the block factors of the last sweep, raspen_jvp! and raspen_jacobian!. RASPEN needs converged local solves.
  • Multiplicative(RAS(partition), Newton()) is the composition of the same map with one Newton step, the nonlinear restricted additive Schwarz preconditioning of Cai and Li (2011).
  • With an inner method other than RAS, the derivative of $r^\ell$ is a central difference.
julia> raspen = LeftPreconditioned(Newton(), RAS(part; max_iterations = 20, rtol = 1e-12));

julia> x, status = solve(prob, zeros(n), raspen);

julia> status.code === SUCCESS && isapprox(x, x_ras; atol = 1e-7)
true

julia> x, status = solve(prob, zeros(n), Multiplicative(RAS(part; max_iterations = 2), Newton()));

julia> status.code === SUCCESS && isapprox(x, x_ras; atol = 1e-7)
true

The status of a preconditioned solve is that of the global residual $F$ at the returned iterate.

How to choose

A combinator changes the convergence region and the cost of a step, and the right choice depends on the problem. Some conditions under which each one applies:

  • Switch and Multiplicative with a cheap first member apply where Newton alone does not converge from the start but converges near the solution.
  • Anderson and Broyden apply to a fixed-point iteration whose map is cheap and whose Jacobian is expensive or unavailable.
  • Additive with OptimalWeights combines methods whose steps are good in different directions.
  • RAS and LeftPreconditioned apply to problems with local nonlinearities that a partition into blocks isolates, where a global Newton step is slowed by those blocks.
